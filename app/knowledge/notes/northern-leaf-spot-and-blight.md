Northern Corn Leaf Spot (NCLS)
Synthesized from PMC4363367 (Liu et al., 2015) and Cornell CALS Extension.
Causal Agent & Pathogen Races
NCLS is caused by the fungus Bipolaris zeicola (anamorph of Cochliobolus carbonum). It infects leaves, sheaths, husks, and ears, and has become more prevalent in no-till systems. Five pathogenic races (Races 0–4) exist:
    •    Race 0: Infects grasses; does not infect maize.
    •    Race 1: Produces host-specific HC toxin and can kill susceptible plants at any growth stage. Rare in modern U.S. hybrids due to widespread deployment of the Hm1 resistance gene, though still problematic in regions lacking resistant germplasm (such as parts of Sichuan Province, China).
    •    Race 2: Widespread globally, but rarely causes economic damage.
    •    Race 3: The primary threat in the U.S. Northeast and Appalachian region (including New York, Pennsylvania, and North Carolina).
    •    Race 4: Produces leaf spots specifically on inbreds with a B73 genetic background.
Symptoms & Disease Progression
    •    Timing: Typically appears around silking (R1) through full maturity, though early-season infection occurs under high disease pressure.
    •    Appearance: Starts as small, circular-to-oval reddish-brown or tan spots. As they mature, lesions turn tan to grayish-tan with a dark pigmented border, measuring 1/8 to 1/2 inch.
    •    "String of Pearls" Pattern: Lesions frequently form in linear rows running parallel along the leaf veins, giving a characteristic "string of pearls" appearance.
    •    Host Molecular Response (PMC4363367): Transcriptome profiling comparing resistant inbred Mo17 against susceptible Zheng58 identified 466 differentially expressed genes across four infection stages (contact, penetration, incubation, and disease periods). Resistance involves upregulating defense enzymes (SOD, POD, PPO, CAT, and PAL), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and plant hormone signal transduction while suppressing pathogen-exploited RNA transport and purine metabolism.
Epidemiology & Management
    •    Disease Cycle: Overwinters on surface corn residue; airborne spores travel long distances via wind and rain splash during warm (68°F–90°F), humid weather.
    •    Control: Planting resistant hybrids is the primary defense, supported by crop rotation and tillage to bury infected residue.
2. Northern Corn Leaf Blight (NCLB)
Synthesized from Purdue Extension (BP-84-W, Wise) and University of Delaware Extension (Oct 2025, Kleczewski & Betts).
Causal Agent & Symptoms
NCLB is caused by the fungus Exserohilum turcicum. Symptoms typically begin on lower leaves and progress upward into the canopy, leaf sheaths, and ear husks:
    •    Early Stage: Long, narrow, tan streaks running parallel to leaf veins.
    •    Diagnostic Mature Lesion: Expands into large, oblong, "cigar-shaped" tan-to-gray lesions measuring 1 to 7 inches long. Under severe pressure, lesions coalesce and blight large sections of foliage.
    •    Fruiting Structures: In high humidity, tiny dark-green to black fungal spores form inside mature lesions (visible with a hand lens), giving them a dirty appearance.
    •    Resistance Phenotypes: Hybrids with partial (polygenic) resistance develop fewer, smaller lesions with reduced sporulation. Hybrids with race-specific Ht genes display small, yellow chlorotic lesions that produce no spores.
Epidemiology & Yield Impact
    •    Disease Cycle: Overwinters in surface corn residue. In spring, spores splash or blow onto lower leaves. Infection requires moderate temperatures (64°F–84°F) and 6 to 18 hours of continuous leaf wetness (heavy dew, overcast days, bottomlands). New lesions appear 7 to 14 days after infection. Hot, dry weather halts disease progression.
    •    Agronomic Impact: Loss of photosynthetic leaf area on or above the ear leaf during the two weeks before and after tasseling causes the highest damage—reducing dent corn yields by 30% to 50%, degrading silage/sweet corn quality, and predisposing plants to stalk rot and lodging.
Integrated Management
    •    Hybrid Selection: Plant hybrids with strong partial resistance ratings or appropriate Ht race-specific resistance genes in fields with a history of NCLB.
    •    Crop Rotation: Rotate out of corn for 1 year (or 2 years in continuous no-till fields) to soybeans, small grains, or vegetables. Avoid sorghum and sudangrass, which act as alternate hosts for E. turcicum.
    •    Residue Management: Tillage accelerates residue decomposition and reduces local inoculum; clean tillage equipment before moving between fields.
    •    Fungicides: Scout fields around V14 (prior to tassel). If disease pressure is high and wet, moderate weather is forecasted, foliar fungicides applied between VT and R3 (especially VT–R1) offer the highest economic return by protecting the ear leaf.
