# Phaeosphaeria leaf spot: notes provided by the user (15 Nov 2026)

Phaeosphaeria Leaf Spot (PLS)—also frequently referred to in literature and regions like Brazil as Maize White Spot (MWS)—is a foliar disease affecting corn (Zea mays).
    •    Historical Taxonomy: The disease has traditionally been attributed to the fungus Phaeosphaeria maydis (anamorph: Phyllosticta sp. or Phoma sp.).
    •    Taxonomic Complexity & Current Research: The exact identity and etiology of the causal organism(s) remain complex and unresolved in plant pathology. While P. maydis is historically cited, other pathogens—including the bacterium Pantoea ananatis and various Phoma / phomoid species—have been implicated in producing identical white-spot symptoms, leading to debate over whether the disease is caused by a single fungus, a fungal complex, or a bacterial-fungal interaction.
    •    2026 U.S. Outbreak Investigation: In September 2026, Michigan State University (MSU) researchers reported an emerging corn leaf spot closely resembling PLS/MWS across approximately 16 Michigan counties, with additional reports in New York and Pennsylvania. Preliminary DNA sequencing has not yet provided enough resolution to confirm the exact species, though researchers consistently recovered a phomoid fungus producing tiny black fruiting bodies (pycnidia) within the lesions.
Symptoms & Disease Progression
PLS/MWS is distinct from other common corn foliar diseases (like Gray Leaf Spot or Northern Corn Leaf Blight) due to its bleached, rounded lesions:
    •    Early Stage: Small, water-soaked, pale green or chlorotic spots appear on the leaves, typically starting on the lower foliage and progressing upward.
    •    Mature Lesions: The spots expand into round, oval, or slightly oblong lesions (roughly 0.3 to 2 cm in diameter). The centers dry out and become bleached, turning white to light tan/straw-colored, often surrounded by a distinct dark brown or reddish-brown margin.
    •    Fruiting Bodies: Under magnification, small black specks (pycnidia or perithecia) are often visible embedded within the necrotic, white centers of older lesions.
    •    Coalescence: Under severe disease pressure, individual lesions merge (coalesce), causing widespread blighting, premature leaf senescence, and severe reductions in photosynthetic area.
Epidemiology & Favorable Conditions
    •    Climate: PLS thrives in cool to moderate temperatures (roughly 14°C–20°C / 57°F–68°F) paired with high relative humidity (above 60–70%), frequent rainfall, or heavy morning dews. Because of this, it is historically most severe in higher-elevation tropical and subtropical regions (such as parts of Brazil, Mexico, and East/Southern Africa), though temperate outbreaks occur when late-summer conditions are cool and wet.
    •    Survival & Spread: The pathogen(s) overwinter in infected corn crop residue on the soil surface. Spores are dispersed to new crops via wind and rain splash.
    •    Susceptible Stages: While infection can occur earlier, symptoms typically become most prominent and damaging from flowering (tasseling/silking) through grain fill.
Agronomic Impact & Management
    •    Yield Loss: When severe infection occurs prior to or during early grain fill, loss of green leaf area can lead to poor kernel fill, reduced grain quality, weakened stalks (predisposing plants to lodging), and significant yield reductions (historically reported between 10% and over 60% in susceptible hybrids under ideal disease conditions).
    •    Genetic Resistance: Planting resistant or tolerant hybrids is the primary and most effective management strategy in regions where the disease is endemic.
    •    Cultural Practices: Crop rotation away from continuous corn and tillage practices that bury infected surface residue help reduce initial inoculum levels.
    •    Chemical Control: Foliar fungicides (such as strobilurins/QoIs, triazoles/DMIs, or premixes) are utilized in high-risk regions to protect the ear leaf during reproductive stages, though efficacy can vary depending on whether the primary causal agent in a given region is fungal or bacterial.
